2. Strict scaling and maximal-boundary normalization
----------------------------------------------------

If eighteen unit disks fit in radius R<R*, their center norms are at most
R-1<q.  Feasibility of even two unit disks implies R>=2 by the triangle
inequality, so R-1>0. Scale all centers about the container center by q/(R-1)>1.
Their pair distances become strictly greater than 2 and their norms at
most q.  It suffices to rule out this strict configuration.

Among strict configurations in radius q, choose one with the greatest
possible number b of centers on the boundary.  A maximizing value exists
because the set of attained counts is a nonempty finite subset of
{0,...,18}; no compactness assertion about the strict feasible set is
needed.  Radial dilation until some center reaches q ensures b>=1.

Let sv be a farthest interior center, with |v|=1 and s<q, and put
D=q-4/q=2sqrt(2).  Suppose s>=D.  Replacing sv by qv preserves all strict
pair inequalities.  For any interior center z, |z|<=s and

 |qv-z|^2-|sv-z|^2
   =(q-s)(q+s-2<v,z>) >= (q-s)^2 >0.

For a boundary center z, |z|=q and

 |qv-z|^2-4
   =(q/s)(|sv-z|^2-4)+(q/s)(q-s)(s-D)>0.

This raises b, a contradiction.  Thus every interior radius is less
than D=2sqrt(2).  The algebraic identity qD=q^2-4 follows from
sqrt(12)=2sqrt(3).

There cannot be twelve boundary centers: two consecutive boundary rays
would have angular gap alpha<=2pi/12=pi/6, and

 |x-y|^2=2q^2(1-cos(alpha))
      <=2(8+4sqrt(3))(1-sqrt(3)/2)=4,

contradicting strict separation.  Hence b<=11.
